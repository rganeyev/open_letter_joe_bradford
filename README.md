---

## 1. Objective of the Letter

This letter is not an attempt to revise your exegesis of the religious sources on zakat. Its objective is to clarify the definition of shares and explain why they cannot be treated as sole proprietorship or general partnership for the zakat calculation.

There are fundamental reasons that make the two types of properties distinct legally and economically. Those distinctions bear directly on the fiqh condition of complete ownership/dominion (*al-milk / al-tasarruf*).

---

## 2. The Methodology Under Review

In the book *Simple Zakat Guide* [1], published in 2015, Shaykh Joe Bradford defines zakat for a long-term shareholder not as 2.5% of the market price of the shares, but as 2.5% of the sum of the company’s:

- Cash  
- Receivables  
- Inventory (CRI)

He further simplifies the calculation by approximating it as **2.5% of 30% of the share price**.

This opinion has been highly influential, and similar variations have since been issued:

- Fiqh Council of North America — advising use of book value (Nov 27, 2025) [2]  
- Shariyah Review Bureau — validating the same methodology (Feb 25, 2025) [3]

As a result, many reputable Muslim financial apps now suggest this calculation:

- Wahed [4]  
- Zoya [5]  
- Launchgood [6]  

The argument relies on an analogy between a shareholder and a shoemaker [1]. The shoemaker only pays zakat on CRI and not on productive assets (e.g., a hammer). This calculation is correct **for a shoemaker**.

The underlying assumption is that a shareholder can be treated as a sole proprietor or partner, and that the ruling extends by analogy.

---

## 3. The Core Legal Distinction:  
### Sole Proprietorship / General Partnership vs Shareholder

### 3.1 Partnership

According to the IRS, a sole proprietor is *“someone who owns an unincorporated business by themselves.”*

A partnership is defined as:

> “A relationship between two or more people to do trade or business. Each person contributes money, property, labor or skill, and shares in the profits and losses of the business.”

Partnerships can permit access, liquidation rights, distributions, dissolution remedies, and accounting rights, making “look-through” reasoning more plausible.

---

### 3.2 Shareholder in a Corporation

A corporation is a **separate legal entity** that owns its assets in its own name. A shareholder owns **shares**, not the corporation’s underlying property.

This principle originates from *Salomon v. Salomon & Co. Ltd.* (1897), which established the separation between corporate property and shareholder property.

A shareholder has a financial interest but **no dominion** over corporate assets.

---

### 3.3 Differences in Ownership

Unlike a shoemaker, a shareholder does **not** have:

- The right to take inventory  
- The right to collect receivables  
- The right to dispose of company cash  
- The right to access or use corporate property  

A shareholder **only** has:

- Voting rights (governance)  
- Dividend rights (conditional on board declaration)  
- Residual claim upon liquidation  

This legal structure enables asset partitioning, limited liability, centralized control, and residual claims. Ownership of Apple, Microsoft, or an S&P 500 ETF is ownership of **shares**, not a divisible claim on cash or inventory.

---

## 4. The Accounting Mirror: GAAP / IFRS

Modern accounting reflects the same distinction:

- **Public equity securities**  
  - Measured at fair value under US GAAP (ASC 321)  
  - Measured at fair value under IFRS 9  

- **Equity method investments**  
  - Used when the investor has significant influence  
  - Carrying amount tracks share of net assets (ASC 323 / IAS 28)  

Only in the “significant influence” zone does a book-value view resemble economic reality. For ordinary shareholders, the asset recognized is the **share at fair value**.

---

## 5. Fiqh – Ownership Condition

A key zakat condition is **complete ownership**, defined as:

> “Authority that enables the owner to use the owned thing and get the benefit of all its utilities in a perpetual and exclusive manner.”  
> — *Al Bahr al Raiq*, Vol. 2, p. 218

(See *Fiqh al Zakah*, Vol. I, Dr. Yusuf al-Qardawi [7])

A shareholder can dispose of their **share**, but generally cannot dispose of, demand, or partition corporate cash, receivables, or inventory.

Therefore, dominion attaches to the **security**, not the underlying balance-sheet items.

---

## 6. Applications

### 6.1 Shareholder Paying No Zakat

Franchise companies often hold minimal assets. Using CRI or book-value methods:

- A McDonald’s shareholder (Dec 8) would pay:
  - **0.06% zakat per dollar** (CRI method)
  - **0%** (book-value method)

Dividends are often replaced by stock buybacks [8], meaning shareholders can realize massive returns without zakat liability under this method.

---

### 6.2 Zakat Exceeding Share Value

For bankrupt companies, asset value may exceed enterprise value.

Example:  
- $150 position in FOXO (Dec 8)  
- Zakat due using CRI method: **$12,433**

This exceeds the shareholder’s total wealth (see Figure 1).

---

### 6.3 The 30% Approximation Is Statistically Wrong

In *Simple Zakat Guide*, Shaykh Joe Bradford states that CRI averages 25–30% of market value.

Issues:

- The estimate is from **2015**
- Today’s haircut is closer to **80%** (Figure 3)
- Correlation is weak for most S&P 500 stocks (Figure 2)

---

## 7. Additional Remarks

At a time of record wealth inequality, much of it driven by equity ownership [9], zakat is meant to redistribute wealth.

While unintended, this methodology effectively grants wealthy shareholders a **70% reduction** in zakat obligations.

---

## 8. A Proposed Correction

A dominion-consistent approach:

- **Publicly traded shares**  
  - Zakat at **2.5% of market value** on zakat date  
  - Plus zakatable cash/dividends  
  - Optionally deduct latent capital-gains tax (if paid in cash)

- **True partnerships / significant influence holdings**  
  - Look-through or net-asset-based zakat may be defensible
